# Audience Discovery Guidelines — V0.1

## Purpose

The goal of audience discovery is not simply to produce demographic segments.
The goal is to identify *groups of people or small businesses around which profitable opportunities may exist*.

An audience can be defined by:

- who people are
- what they own
- what they do
- what they are responsible for
- something that recently happened to them
- a recurring event they participate in
- a problem they are currently trying to solve
- a transaction they are making
- a technology/platform they depend on
- a regulatory or environmental change affecting them
- a shared workflow
- a temporary circumstance

Examples:

- Airbnb hosts
- people searching for parts for vintage Porsche models
- adult children managing an elderly parent's home remotely
- volunteer youth-sports travel coordinators
- restaurants operating discontinued commercial equipment
- homeowners in a city receiving new smart water meters
- private pilots visiting unfamiliar regional airports

An audience does *not* have to be large.
A small audience may be attractive if it is identifiable, reachable and economically interesting.

## 1. Do not start with a fixed definition of a "good audience"

Do not require that every audience:

- be large
- have an online community
- complain frequently
- spend a certain amount
- be growing
- fit a particular demographic profile

These may be useful signals, but they are hypotheses rather than requirements.
The system should learn over time which audience characteristics actually lead to profitable opportunities.

## 2. Audiences may be discovered from any direction

Audience discovery should not always begin by asking:

> "What groups of people should we study?"

An audience may emerge after discovering something else.

Possible starting points include:

### A change

Example: A city introduces a new utility technology.
Ask:

> Who is affected by this change?

### A problem

Example: People repeatedly struggle to determine whether obsolete parts fit old equipment.
Ask:

> Which groups experience this problem most intensely?

### An unusual behavior

Example: People repeatedly create spreadsheets to coordinate the same recurring activity.
Ask:

> Who does this, and why?

### Existing spending

Example: A group spends significant money across fragmented suppliers.
Ask:

> Who controls this spending, and what friction exists around it?

### Poor existing products

Example: A product category has consistently bad reviews around one issue.
Ask:

> Which customers care most about that issue?

### An event

Example: Families repeatedly travel for weekend tournaments.
Ask:

> Who carries the operational burden of that event?

### A technology capability

Example: A new technology makes something possible that used to require an expert.
Ask:

> Who previously could not afford or access this capability?

## 3. Think in terms of "who + circumstance"

Avoid assuming an audience is just a demographic label.

Instead of:

> Vintage car owners

the useful audience may become:

> Vintage car owners currently trying to source a hard-to-find replacement part.

Instead of:

> People with elderly parents

it may become:

> Adult children who live far away but are responsible for maintaining a parent's home.

Instead of:

> Youth sports parents

it may become:

> Volunteer team managers coordinating several overnight tournaments per season.

An audience may become more specific as evidence emerges.
Do not force specificity before the system has learned enough.

## 4. Start broad, then allow evidence to segment

It is acceptable to begin with:

> Airbnb hosts

The system should then look for evidence that certain subgroups behave differently.

Possible segmentation dimensions:

- frequency
- spending
- number of assets/customers
- geography
- experience level
- type of problem
- urgency
- ownership versus rental
- professional versus hobbyist
- technology usage
- event frequency
- responsibility
- willingness to delegate
- product currently used

Do not segment simply because segmentation is possible.
Segment when evidence suggests that a subgroup has meaningfully different:

- needs
- economics
- reach
- behavior
- willingness to pay
- product fit

## 5. Merge audiences when the underlying opportunity is shared

Audience discovery should work in both directions.
The system may split an audience.
It may also discover that apparently unrelated audiences share the same problem.

Example:

- vintage-car owners
- restaurant owners with old ovens
- farmers with old equipment

may all share:

> Difficulty sourcing and verifying obsolete replacement parts.

At that point, the useful audience may become defined by the *situation*, not the original category.

## 6. Reach is part of audience understanding

Finding an interesting group is not enough.
The system should investigate:

> *How can we actually observe or reach these people?*

For every promising audience, identify potential *Reach Surfaces*.

Examples:

- Reddit communities
- Facebook groups
- forums
- newsletters
- industry associations
- marketplaces
- search queries
- creators/influencers
- directories
- public records
- events
- physical locations
- mailing lists
- phone lists
- clubs
- service providers
- transaction platforms
- employers
- channel partners
- local advertising
- direct mail
- billboards
- temporary owned audiences

Reach does not need to be digital.
However, whenever possible, responses should be measurable digitally.

## 7. Reach should be evaluated structurally

Do not merely say:

> "This audience is reachable on Facebook."

Ask:

- Can we identify individual members?
- Can we target them?
- Can we observe them without contacting them?
- Can we contact them directly?
- Can we acquire one gatekeeper who reaches many of them?
- What does reaching them cost?
- Are there restrictions?
- Is there intent data available?
- Can we measure responses?
- Is reach concentrated or fragmented?
- Does someone already aggregate this audience?

An audience with 5,000 identifiable people may be more attractive than one with 5 million people who are difficult to reach.

## 8. Look for concentrated access

Pay particular attention to situations where reaching *one person unlocks many customers*.

Examples:

- team coordinator → 20 families
- HOA manager → 300 homeowners
- club president → 500 members
- tournament organizer → thousands of participants
- accountant → hundreds of small businesses
- specialty supplier → many equipment owners

This can radically change acquisition economics.

## 9. Listen broadly for signals

Do not equate signal with complaint.

Within an audience, search for:

- Pain — "I hate doing this."
- Workarounds — People building spreadsheets, using WhatsApp, hiring assistants, or inventing manual processes.
- Spending — What do they already buy?
- Repetition — What happens over and over?
- Mistakes — Where do people lose money or time?
- Fear / uncertainty — Where are they afraid of making the wrong decision?
- Trust problems — Where do they need expertise they do not possess?
