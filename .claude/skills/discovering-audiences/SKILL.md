# Discovering Audiences

The goal of audience discovery is **not** to produce demographic segments. It is to identify **groups of people or small businesses around which a profitable opportunity may exist**. An audience does not have to be large — a small audience is attractive if it is *identifiable, reachable, and economically interesting*.

The full reference this skill operationalizes lives at `docs/audience-discovery-guidelines.md`. Read it when you need the complete rationale; this file is the working procedure.

## Guiding principle: no fixed definition of a "good" audience

Do not require that an audience be large, have an online community, complain frequently, spend a certain amount, be growing, or fit a demographic profile. These are **useful signals, not requirements** — treat them as hypotheses. Let evidence, not a checklist, decide whether an audience is worth pursuing.

## An audience can be defined by any of these

- who people are
- what they own
- what they do
- what they are responsible for
- something that recently happened to them
- a recurring event they participate in
- a problem they are currently trying to solve
- a transaction they are making
- a technology/platform they depend on
- a regulatory or environmental change affecting them
- a shared workflow
- a temporary circumstance

## Procedure

### 1. Pick a starting point — from ANY direction

Do not always start from "what groups should we study?" An audience often emerges *after* you notice something else. Any of these is a valid entry point; whichever you start from, pivot to the audience question next to it:

| Starting point | Pivot question |
|---|---|
| A change (e.g. a city rolls out new utility tech) | Who is affected by this change? |
| A problem (e.g. hard to tell if obsolete parts fit old equipment) | Which groups experience this problem most intensely? |
| An unusual behavior (e.g. people build spreadsheets to coordinate a recurring activity) | Who does this, and why? |
| Existing spending (e.g. money spread across fragmented suppliers) | Who controls this spending, and what friction surrounds it? |
| A poor existing product (consistently bad reviews on one issue) | Which customers care most about that issue? |
| An event (e.g. families travel for weekend tournaments) | Who carries the operational burden of that event? |
| A new technology capability (something once needing an expert) | Who previously could not afford or access this? |

### 2. Frame the audience as "who + circumstance"

A demographic label is rarely the useful unit. Add the circumstance that creates the opportunity:

- "Vintage car owners" → *Vintage car owners currently trying to source a hard-to-find replacement part.*
- "People with elderly parents" → *Adult children who live far away but are responsible for maintaining a parent's home.*
- "Youth sports parents" → *Volunteer team managers coordinating several overnight tournaments per season.*

Let specificity increase **as evidence emerges** — do not force it prematurely.

### 3. Segment only when evidence justifies it

It is fine to begin broad (e.g. "Airbnb hosts"), then look for subgroups that behave differently. Candidate dimensions: frequency, spending, number of assets/customers, geography, experience level, type of problem, urgency, ownership vs. rental, professional vs. hobbyist, technology usage, event frequency, responsibility, willingness to delegate, product currently used.

Do **not** segment just because you can. Split only when evidence shows a subgroup differs meaningfully in needs, economics, reach, behavior, willingness to pay, or product fit.

### 4. Merge when the underlying opportunity is shared

Discovery runs both directions. Apparently unrelated audiences (vintage-car owners, restaurants with old ovens, farmers with old equipment) may share one problem (*sourcing and verifying obsolete replacement parts*). When they do, the useful audience may be defined by the **situation**, not the original category.

### 5. Map reach surfaces

Finding an interesting group is not enough — ask *how can we actually observe or reach these people?* For every promising audience, list concrete Reach Surfaces. Reach need not be digital, but prefer surfaces where responses are **measurable digitally**. Examples to consider: Reddit communities, Facebook groups, forums, newsletters, industry associations, marketplaces, search queries, creators/influencers, directories, public records, events, physical locations, mailing/phone lists, clubs, service providers, transaction platforms, employers, channel partners, local advertising, direct mail, billboards, temporary owned audiences.

### 6. Evaluate reach structurally

Do not settle for "they're on Facebook." For each surface, answer:

- Can we identify individual members?
- Can we target them?
- Can we observe them without contacting them?
- Can we contact them directly?
- Can we acquire one gatekeeper who reaches many of them?
- What does reaching them cost?
- Are there restrictions?
- Is intent data available?
- Can we measure responses?
- Is reach concentrated or fragmented?
- Does someone already aggregate this audience?

An audience of 5,000 identifiable, reachable people can beat 5,000,000 who are hard to reach.

### 7. Look for concentrated access (gatekeepers)

Prioritize situations where reaching **one person unlocks many customers** — it can transform acquisition economics: team coordinator → ~20 families; HOA manager → ~300 homeowners; club president → ~500 members; tournament organizer → thousands of participants; accountant → hundreds of small businesses; specialty supplier → many equipment owners.

### 8. Listen broadly for signals (not just complaints)

Signal is not the same as complaint. Within an audience, search for:

- **Pain** — "I hate doing this."
- **Workarounds** — spreadsheets, WhatsApp threads, hired assistants, manual processes.
- **Spending** — what do they already buy?
- **Repetition** — what happens over and over?
- **Mistakes** — where do people lose money or time?
- **Fear / uncertainty** — where are they afraid of a wrong decision?
- **Trust problems** — where do they need expertise they lack?
- **Search** — what are they actively looking for?

## Output: an audience profile

For each audience you surface, produce:

1. **Audience statement** — one line in "who + circumstance" form.
2. **How it was discovered** — the starting point (change / problem / behavior / spending / poor product / event / capability).
3. **Why it may be economically interesting** — the opportunity hypothesis.
4. **Segmentation notes** — subgroups, and the evidence that justifies (or doesn't yet justify) splitting.
5. **Shared-situation links** — other audiences that may share the same underlying problem.
6. **Reach surfaces** — each with its structural evaluation from step 6.
7. **Concentrated access** — any gatekeeper who unlocks many members.
8. **Signals observed** — categorized per step 8, with evidence/sources.
9. **Open questions & next evidence to gather** — what would confirm or kill this audience.

Keep every claim tied to evidence, and flag which parts are hypothesis versus observed.

## Anti-patterns to avoid

- Starting every search from "what groups should we study?" and nothing else.
- Treating a demographic label as a finished audience.
- Requiring size, an online community, or frequent complaints before an audience "counts."
- Segmenting because segmentation is possible rather than because evidence demands it.
- Declaring an audience "reachable" without a structural reach evaluation.
- Equating signal with complaint and ignoring workarounds, spending, repetition, and search.
